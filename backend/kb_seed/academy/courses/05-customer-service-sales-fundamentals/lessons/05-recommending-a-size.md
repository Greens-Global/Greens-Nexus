## What You'll Be Able to Do

- Recommend a unit size from what the customer is storing, and explain why in one sentence.
- Use the sizing follow-up questions and the size guide.
- Know when to go up a size, and how to handle a size that is sold out.
- Spot climate-sensitive items and recommend climate control only when it helps.
- Screen for items that cannot be stored, kindly, during discovery.
- Offer protection, locks, boxes and autopay in a way the customer will thank you for.

## Why Not "What Size Do You Need?"

Picture a first-time renter. They have never stood inside a 10x10. They do not know a unit has about an 8-foot ceiling. They have no idea whether a sofa stands on end. When you ask "What size do you need?" you hand them a decision they cannot make, and they guess. They guess small, because small is cheaper. Then on moving day, half their things do not fit, and they are angry at you.

Both of the trade trainers this lesson draws on (Ballard and Maxwell in 2011, DeFazio in 2026) teach the same rule: **ask what they are storing, never what size they need.** You are the expert. Recommending a size is a service, not a sales trick.

## The Sizing Questions

Start with "What are you planning to store?" Then:

1. **"From what size home? About how many rooms?"** Rooms are the fastest way to a size.
2. **"Any big pieces, like a sectional, a piano, a king bed or a large dining table?"** Start from the largest item and make sure it fits.
3. **"Will you need to get to things often?"** Frequent access means room for a walkway, and a ground-floor or drive-up unit.
4. **"Anything that doesn't like heat?"** See climate control below.
5. **"About how many boxes?"** People always underestimate. Add a third.

For vehicles, the questions are different: overall length including the tongue or hitch, height to the highest point, width with mirrors and slide-outs, and how often they take it out. RV & Boat Storage Fundamentals, Measuring for the Right Fit covers the fit check in full.

|---|---|---|---|
| 5x5 | 25 | A hall closet | Boxes, seasonal items, a small dresser |
| 5x10 | 50 | A walk-in closet | A studio's furniture, a mattress set, boxes |

| 10x20 | 200 | A one-car garage | A three-bedroom house, or a car |
| 10x30 | 300 | A large garage | A four- to five-bedroom house |
| Parking | Varies | - | Measure overall length including the tongue and trailer, plus height for covered or enclosed spaces |

(Size guides from SpareFoot, 01/2026, CubeSmart and U-Haul, which broadly agree.)

The 10x10 is the most rented size in the industry, and it is Escondido's and Valley Center's most common size too: 202 and 203 units (From Greens' Cubby, 11/2026). Know your site's 10x10 rate.

### Putting Things in the Unit, Out Loud

Customers trust a recommendation they can picture. Place their things in the unit for them:

> [!NOTE]
> "In a 10x15, your sofa goes along the back wall standing on end, the bed frame and mattress beside it, the dresser on the left, and the boxes stacked on the right up to about head height. That leaves you a walkway down the middle to get to the kitchen boxes."

Other visual aids: the size guide on the website, a sized-box display in the office if your site has one, and best of all, an empty unit (the Walk-Ins and the Tour lesson).

## When in Doubt, Go Up a Size

DeFazio's 2026 advice: **when you are unsure, go up a size.** A customer who doubts their things will fit goes and shops one more place. A customer who is told "this will fit, with room to spare" stops shopping.

Going up a size is not upselling if you explain why:

> [!NOTE]
> "You could probably squeeze it into a 10x10, but you said you'll need to get to your work files every week. In a 10x15 you'll have a walkway, so you're not unstacking everything to reach one box."

**If the right size is sold out:** offer the next size up. One trade source suggests offering it at the smaller unit's price; at Greens, that is a pricing decision above your authority, so ask your manager before you offer it. Never squeeze a customer into a unit that is too small just to make the rental.

**On a tour**, show the smaller size first and then the one you recommend. People understand size by comparison.

## Climate-Sensitive Items

At Greens, climate-controlled units are at Escondido: 549 of its 717 enclosed units (From Greens' Cubby, 11/2026). At the other sites, drive-up units are the main enclosed product (Valley Center has 207 drive-up units, From Greens' Cubby, 11/2026).

Ask about heat when the customer mentions any of these:

| Item | Why heat or humidity hurts it |
|---|---|
| Wood furniture, antiques | Warps, cracks and loosens joints |
| Pianos and instruments | Go out of tune, crack, glue fails |
| Electronics | Heat and condensation damage parts |
| Photos, documents, books | Curl, stick together, fade |
| Artwork | Paint and frames crack |
| Mattresses and upholstered furniture | Hold moisture, smell, invite pests |
| Vinyl records, film, collectibles | Warp and degrade |

Inland California and Texas summers are hot. For these items, climate control is a real need, not a pitch. For lawn furniture, tools, holiday decorations and boxes of clothes, it usually is not, and **pushing climate control for things that do not need it** is one of the add-ons a customer resents later.

## Screening for What Cannot Be Stored

"What are you planning to store?" also screens for things that cannot be stored. Self-Storage Fundamentals, The Rental Agreement lists them. The common ones in a sales call:

- Anything flammable or hazardous: gasoline cans, propane tanks, paint thinner, chemicals.
- Food and perishables (they bring pests).
- Anything living: plants and animals.
- Tires and batteries, and anything else the rental agreement lists.

Ask it as a helpful check, not an accusation:

> [!NOTE]
> "Just so you know, we can't take anything flammable, like gas cans or propane tanks, or any food, because of pests. Is any of that in the mix? ... Great, then a 10x15 is perfect."

Discovery also spots **business renters** (storing inventory or tools is fine; running a business out of the unit is not) and **anyone who mentions staying in the unit**, which is never allowed. Explain the rule kindly now, not at move-in.

## Add-Ons That Help

DeFazio's test for any add-on: **"Would the customer be upset later to learn you hadn't offered something that could have helped?"** If yes, offer it. If no, leave it.

| Add-on | How to offer it | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **Protection** | Lead with a real situation, not a disclosure: a leak from a neighboring unit, a fire nearby. "Stories close; disclosures don't" (DeFazio, 2026). Then the choice: Greens' plan or their own policy. Never call the plan "insurance" and never say it is required unless the agreement says so. | Greens Tenant Protection |
| **Lock** | Recommend a disc or cylinder lock made for storage and show why it is hard to cut. | Self-Storage Fundamentals, Access, Locks and Security |
| **Boxes and supplies** | Offer at every rental. A trade mystery-shop standard (Mixon, 2020). Bundle a starter kit and offer to carry it to the car. | |
| **Autopay** | "This way you never have to think about it, and you'll never get hit with a late fee." | Cubby Fundamentals, Take a Payment |
| **A larger unit** | When their things will likely outgrow the smaller one, or the stay is long. | |

**What to avoid:**

- Stacking add-ons before the customer has agreed to rent. Recommend the unit, close, then add what helps.
- Implying protection is required when it is not, or calling a protection plan "insurance."
- Pushing climate control for items that do not need it.

> [!WARNING]
> Training only, not legal advice. When in doubt, stop and ask your manager.

Selling insurance can require a license, and a protection plan is a different product from insurance (Self-Storage Fundamentals, Tenant Protection and Insurance). Describe Greens' plan only with the approved wording on Greens Tenant Protection.

## Greens Scenarios

**Valley Center.** A caller asks for "your cheapest unit." You ask what she is storing: a one-bedroom apartment and a bicycle. You recommend a 10x10, place her things in it out loud, and mention a drive-up unit since she is moving it herself.

**Escondido.** A man is storing his late father's antique desk and a box of old photographs. You ask whether they would mind the heat, explain what heat does to wood and photos, and recommend a climate-controlled unit. He thanks you; he had not thought of it.

**Escondido.** A caller wants a 5x10 for "a couch, a bed, a table, maybe 40 boxes." You tell her honestly that it will not fit and why, and recommend a 10x15. She had been about to rent a 5x10 down the road.

**Fairfield.** A man wants to park a 30-foot travel trailer. You ask the length with the tongue: 34 feet. You recommend a space with room to spare rather than an exact fit (RV & Boat Storage Fundamentals, Measuring for the Right Fit).

**Temecula.** A contractor mentions he will keep "a couple of gas cans for the generator" in his unit. You explain kindly that fuel cannot be stored and why, and he agrees to keep them at home.

**A Texas site.** A family storing a whole house mentions a piano. Texas summers are hot. You ask whether the site has climate-controlled units, check the Site Profile, and recommend one for the piano and the wood furniture.

## Key Points

- Never ask "What size do you need?" Ask what they are storing, then recommend and say why.
- Follow-ups: home size and rooms, the biggest pieces, how often they need access, anything heat-sensitive, how many boxes.
- Use the size guide as a start: 10x10 for a one-bedroom, 10x15 for a two-bedroom, 10x20 for a three-bedroom. The 10x10 is the most common size at Escondido and Valley Center (From Greens' Cubby, 11/2026).
- When in doubt, go up a size. Never squeeze someone into a unit that is too small.
- Recommend climate control for wood, instruments, electronics, photos, documents, art and mattresses, not for everything. At Greens, it is at Escondido.
- Screen for flammables, food, living things and other prohibited items kindly during discovery.
- Offer protection, a lock, supplies and autopay because they help. Never call the plan insurance or imply it is required.
