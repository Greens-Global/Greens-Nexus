## The Phone Is Still the Sale

Most customers look online first. Then they pick up the phone. A 2026 dataset from an AI phone vendor (Swivl, published 09/28/2026) credited voice calls with 88% to 91% of the rentals it tracked. Websites and online rentals are growing (lesson 10), but for now the phone is where most rentals are decided.

And a lot of the calls are not new customers at all. One vendor claims up to 80% of incoming calls come from existing tenants. That number is not well sourced, but every site manager will tell you most calls are "what's my balance," "my code doesn't work," and "what are your hours." Each of those calls is a chance, either to keep a tenant happy or to find out the caller is actually shopping.

## What Mystery Shoppers Found

The best study we have is old, and it is still the best. In 2004, IntelliShop placed about 1,000 mystery-shop calls to more than 500 storage facilities and graded what staff did (Ron Welty, Inside Self-Storage, 12/2004). Here is what they found:

| What staff did | How often |
|---|---|
| Answered professionally | 92% |
| Asked about the caller's needs | 94.9% |
| Invited the caller to visit | 98% |
| Asked to set an appointment time | 33% |
| Used the property name at the close | 39.8% |
| **Asked the caller to rent** | **25.4%** |
| Offered to send follow-up information | 21% |
| Gave no information about features | 22% |

And one more finding: 87% of shoppers said they would rent from the person they spoke to, based on how comfortable that person made them feel.

Read that table again. **Staff were friendly. They just did not sell.** Nine in ten were professional, but only one in four asked for the rental. Two thirds never offered a set time to visit.

Newer, less formal reports say not much has changed. A 2026 operator column (DeFazio, 06/16/2026) said many managers do not answer at all, that roughly 95% of those who do fail to capture the caller's contact details, and that reservations are almost never offered. Jordheim wrote in 2015 that about 90% of the time, a caller who asks "How late are you open?" gets the hours and nothing else: nobody asks why they called.

**This is where Greens wins.** Being friendly is common. Asking good questions, asking for the rental, capturing contact details and following up are rare. Do those four things and you will beat most of the sites your customers call.

## How Many Calls Should Become Rentals

You will hear numbers thrown around. Here are the real ones, with their years:

| Benchmark | What it says | Source and year |
|---|---|---|
| About 35% | "The average conversion rate of calls to sales in this industry is 35 percent" | Sue Weinman, Inside Self-Storage, 04/15/2010. Still repeated today, but it is from 2010 |
| About 15% of sales calls; about 4% of all touchpoints | 38 rentals from 252 sales calls; about 4% when every inbound call, chat and web visit is counted | Swivl (AI phone vendor), 09/28/2026 |
| About 20% | Framed as a rate most sites should improve on | StoragePug (marketing vendor), undated blog |

Why are these so different? **The denominator.** If you count only calls from people who want to rent, the rate is higher. If you count every call, including "what's my balance," it is much lower. When Greens measures you, it will count new-rental calls only: people who called about renting a unit or a space.

A fair target for Greens today: **aim for at least 35% of new-rental calls ending in a rental or a reservation**, and measure call-to-reservation and reservation-to-move-in separately. Greens will set its own target from Cubby data as it builds history.

> [!NOTE]
> You may see a claim online that "phone conversion fell from 65% to 57%." We could not trace it to any study, and it looks like a mix-up with the "57% rent from the first site they call" figure from lesson 1. Do not repeat it.

### What a Missed Call Costs

Weinman's 2010 example: a site that gets 600 rental calls a year and converts 35% makes 210 rentals. If it misses 15% of its calls, it loses about 32 of those rentals, which she put at about $41,000 a year in revenue at the rents of the time.

A Greens version with today's numbers: if a missed call costs one Valley Center rental at the site's median enclosed street rate of $129 a month (From Greens' Cubby, 11/2026), and that tenant would have stayed about 20 months, that one missed call cost about $2,580 in rent. Miss two a week and the number gets serious fast.

## Speed to Answer

- **Pick up by the third ring.** Weinman's 2010 studies found many storage calls go to voicemail or are answered after three rings. Callers who hear five rings hang up and call the next site on the list.
- **Missed calls are a warning sign.** A 2026 vendor (Storiq) says a missed-call rate above 10% during business hours "warrants attention." Some calls arrive after hours: one vendor puts it at 15% to 20%, unverified.
- **If you are with a customer at the counter**, it is fine to say: "Excuse me one second." Then pick up: "Thank you for calling Greens Storage Escondido, this is Ana. I'm with a customer. May I get your name and number and call you back within 15 minutes?" Then do it.

## Every Call Gets a "Why"

The fastest way to turn an ordinary call into a rental is to ask why they called. It works for hours, prices and directions.

**The hours question**

> [!NOTE]
> Caller: "How late are you open?"
>
> You: "Our office is open until [closing time] today, and gate access runs until [gate closing time]. Are you looking at renting a unit, or can I help you with your account?"

**The price question**

> [!NOTE]
> Caller: "How much is a 10x10?"
>
> You: "Our 10x10s run [low] to [high] depending on whether it's climate controlled or drive-up. What are you planning to store? I want to make sure a 10x10 is even the right size for you."

**The directions question**

> [!NOTE]
> Caller: "Where exactly are you?"
>
> You: "We're at [address], near [landmark]. Are you coming out to see a unit? I'd love to have one ready for you. What time works?"

Each of these answers the question first (never dodge it), then asks one question that opens the conversation. Lesson 4 picks up from there.

## Voicemail and Callbacks

### Your Outgoing Message

It should say: the property name, office hours and gate hours, the website for renting online, and a promise of a callback.

> [!NOTE]
> "Thank you for calling Greens Storage Temecula. We're with other customers right now. Our office is open [office hours] and the gate is open [gate hours]. You can also rent online at [website]. Please leave your name and number and we'll call you back within the hour."

### Leaving a Message for a Prospect

Say your first name, the property, the unit and the price they asked about, your direct number, and when you will try again.

> [!NOTE]
> "Hi Joe, this is Ana with Greens Storage Murrieta. You asked about a pull-through space for your fifth wheel. I have one that fits, and I'd love to hold it for you. Call me at [number]. I'll also try you again tomorrow around 10:00 AM."

Then log the attempt against the lead in Cubby, and call again at 10:00 AM.

### What Never Goes in a Voicemail

**Never leave a balance, a late fee, a lien or any collections detail on a voicemail or with whoever answers.** Someone else may hear it. Lesson 13 covers the rules; for collections voicemails you leave only your name, Greens, and a callback number.

## Overflow and After-Hours Calls

Many storage operators roll overflow and after-hours calls to a storage call center or an AI voice agent. Cubby can take calls with its own voice agent; when it does, the lead's source is "AI Voice Agent" (Cubby Fundamentals, Leads and Reservations). Whatever Greens uses at your site, the rule is the same: **a lead or reservation taken by someone else is yours the moment you see it.** Call the customer, confirm the size fits, and confirm the move-in date.

## Recorded Calls

Reviewing your own recorded calls is the fastest way to get better (lesson 6). If your line records calls, the law decides what the caller must be told.

> [!WARNING]
> Training only, not legal advice. When in doubt, stop and ask your manager.

- **California** requires the consent of everyone on the call to record it (Penal Code 632). A recorded line needs a "this call may be recorded" notice at the start.
- **Texas** allows recording with the consent of one person on the call.
- Greens decides whether and how calls are recorded. Never record a call on your own phone.

## Greens Scenarios

**Escondido.** A caller asks your hours. You give them, then ask whether she is looking to rent. She is: her apartment lease ends in two weeks. Without that one question, she would have hung up and called the next site.

**Fairfield.** Three calls ring while you are walking the lot. You let one go to voicemail. You call it back within the hour from the lot: it was a man looking for a 32-foot space for a trailer.

**Temecula.** A caller asks for "your cheapest parking space." You give the low end of the range, then ask what he is parking. It is a 26-foot boat with a trailer. Now you can actually help.

**Valley Center.** You leave a prospect a voicemail with your name, the unit and price she asked about, your number and when you will call again. She calls back that afternoon.

**A Texas site.** A tenant calls about her balance. Before you hang up, you ask whether there is anything else you can help with. She mentions her brother needs a unit next month. You take his name and number.

## Key Points

- Most rentals are still decided on the phone (Swivl, 2026).
- In the 2004 IntelliShop study, 92% of staff answered professionally but only 25.4% asked the caller to rent and 33% asked to set an appointment. Friendly is common; selling is rare.
- The 35% calls-to-rentals figure is from 2010; a 2026 vendor dataset found about 15% of sales calls became rentals. The denominator decides the rate. Never repeat the "65% to 57%" claim.
- Pick up by the third ring. Answer every question, then ask why they called.
- Voicemails to prospects: name, property, unit and price, your number, when you will call again. Never a balance or collections detail.
- California needs everyone's consent to record a call (Penal Code 632); Texas needs one person's.
