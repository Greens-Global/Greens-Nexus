## A Building Is Not an Expense

If Greens expensed a $4 million building the year it was bought, that year would show a huge loss and every year after a free building. Neither is true. The building serves for decades, so its cost is spread over the years it serves. That spreading is **depreciation**, and the building, the lot, the canopies, the gate operators and the office computers are **fixed assets** (property, plant and equipment).

The line is the company's **capitalization policy**: a dollar threshold (small purchases are expensed whatever they are) and the test of whether the spend **adds value or extends life** (capital) or **keeps the asset working as it was** (repair). A new roof is capital; patching the roof is repair. A new gate operator is capital; a new sprocket is repair. Accounting applies the policy; the site's job is to describe the work accurately on the ticket and the invoice so the right call can be made.

## Depreciation

**Straight-line depreciation** spreads an asset's cost evenly over its useful life:

| Asset | Cost | Useful life | Yearly depreciation | Monthly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A gate operator | $3,800 | 10 years | $380 | $31.67 |
| A canopy row | $120,000 | 25 years | $4,800 | $400.00 |
| A repave | $90,000 | 15 years | $6,000 | $500.00 |
| A building | $2,400,000 | 39 years | $61,538 | $5,128.21 |
| Land | $1,000,000 | Never | $0 | $0 |

Each month, Accounting records the depreciation of every asset:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Depreciation Expense | the month | |
| Accumulated Depreciation | | the month |

**Accumulated depreciation** is a contra asset: it sits against the asset's cost, so the balance sheet shows the asset's **net book value** (cost less depreciation taken so far). A ten-year-old gate operator that cost $3,800 has a net book value near zero, which is one more reason its replacement is a capital case and not a surprise.

Land is never depreciated; it does not wear out. The building on it does. Tax depreciation follows its own rules and schedules, which is one reason the books and the tax return differ; Accounting keeps both.

## The Fixed Asset Register

Every capitalized asset has a row in the **fixed asset register**: what it is, which property it belongs to, when it went in service, its cost, its life, its accumulated depreciation and its net book value. The register is the accounting twin of the property's records in Asset Management: the warranty record there says when the gate operator went in and who installed it; the register says what it cost and how much of it is left.

While a project is under way, its costs accumulate in **construction in progress**, an asset that does not depreciate yet. When the work is done and the asset is in use (the canopy is up, the operator is running), it is **placed in service**, moved to its asset class, and depreciation begins.

## Improvements Versus Repairs, in Practice

The same vendor visit can be both. A roofer who replaces a whole roof section (capital) and also clears the gutters (repair) sends one invoice; Accounting splits it by the scope on the ticket. That is one more reason the scope of work (the Getting the Work Done lesson) is written line by line: it is what lets the invoice be split correctly.

| The work | Usually |
|---|---|
| Replace all the roof panels on a building | Capital |
| Replace two damaged panels | Repair |
| Repave the lot | Capital |
| Crack seal, sealcoat, stripe | Repair |
| New gate operator | Capital |
| New sprocket, chain, photo eye | Repair |
| New HVAC unit | Capital |
| Compressor replaced in an existing unit | Depends on the policy; often repair |
| New canopy row | Capital |
| Replace a bent canopy post | Repair |

## When an Asset Leaves

- **Replaced**: the old asset's cost and its accumulated depreciation come off the books; whatever net book value is left is a loss (or a gain, if it was sold for more). The new asset goes on.
- **Sold** (a site vehicle, old equipment): the proceeds against the net book value give a gain or a loss.
- **Destroyed** (a storm takes a canopy row): the net book value is written off, and the insurance recovery, when it comes, is recorded against the loss. The photos and the incident record (the Risk, Insurance and Incidents lesson) are what the claim and the write-off both rest on.

## A Greens Scenario

Temecula's gate operator fails for the third time and the capital case is approved. The new operator is bought through a purchase order, installed by the gate vendor and placed in service on 03/14. Accounting adds it to the register at its cost, with a ten-year life, and starts depreciating it in March. The old operator, almost fully depreciated, comes off the register with a small loss. In Asset Management the manager adds the new warranty record with its term and the installer's name. Two records, one asset, each in its home.

## Common Mistakes

- **Expensing a capital purchase** so the budget "looks better." It misstates both this year and every year after.
- **Capitalizing a repair** to spread it out. Same problem, the other way.
- **An invoice with no scope** that cannot be split between capital and repair.
- **A replaced asset left on the register** at its old value.

## Key Points

- A fixed asset serves for years, so its cost is spread over its life through depreciation; a repair is expensed in its period. The capitalization policy decides which.
- Straight-line depreciation is cost divided by life, recorded monthly; accumulated depreciation nets the asset to its book value; land never depreciates.
- Project costs sit in construction in progress until placed in service; the fixed asset register is the accounting twin of Asset Management's records.
- Replaced, sold or destroyed assets come off the books at net book value, with a gain, a loss or an insurance recovery.
