## What You'll Be Able to Do

- Explain how a site's budget is built and read a budget-to-actual report.
- Write a variance comment that says what happened and what, if anything, to do.
- Name the internal controls a site lives inside and why each exists.
- Keep the records an audit will ask for, and know what an auditor is doing.

## The Budget

A **budget** is next year's income statement written in advance, line by line, for each site. Revenue is built from the rent roll (units, rates, expected occupancy and rate changes, concessions); expenses are built from the schedules and contracts (the preventive year, the service contracts, payroll by shift, insurance and property tax from the renewals), plus what the site knows is coming. The Budgets and Capital Planning lesson in the Property & Vendor Management course built the maintenance lines; Accounting assembles the whole.

A budget has two uses: it is the plan leadership approved, and it is the yardstick each month is measured against. A budget line nobody can explain is not a plan; it is a wish.

## Variance Analysis

Each month the **budget-to-actual** report lays the plan beside what happened. The difference is the **variance**, favorable or unfavorable. The report is useless without the comments, and a useful comment answers three questions:


| A comment that helps | A comment that does not |
|---|---|
| "Utilities $900 over: October's bill arrived in November, timing; November alone was on budget." | "Utilities over." |
| "Rental income $2,400 under: two free-month concessions on the new garages, both at full rent from 12/01." | "Promotions." |
| "Bad debt $1,100 over: the over-60 aging grew while two lien notices waited; sold 11/15; the aging is back to normal." | "Delinquency." |

Write the comment from the drill-down, with the ticket numbers, and the next question answers itself.

## Internal Controls

**Internal controls** are the rules and checks that keep the books honest, protect cash and property, and make sure what is reported is what happened. A storage company runs on a small set of them, and every one asks something of the site:

| Control | Why | What it asks of the site |
|---|---|---|
| **Segregation of duties** | One person must not be able to take and hide | The person who takes payments does not reconcile them; the person who approves a vendor does not pay it |
| **Authorization limits** | Spending is approved by the level the amount needs | Approval in writing on the ticket before the work; no split jobs |
| **Three-way matching** | Nothing is paid that was not ordered and received | Ticket numbers on invoices, scopes, inspections |
| **No cash outside the system** | Every payment and refund has a record | Deposits intact, refunds through Cubby and Accounting, no vendor paid from the site |
| **Waivers and credits with a reason** | Revenue reductions are deliberate and traceable | The reason on every waiver, within the limits (Greens Waivers, Credits and Refunds) |
| **Reconciliations** | The books agree with the outside world | Same-day answers to reconciliation questions |
| **Access control** | People see and change only what their job needs | Nexus and Cubby roles, no shared sign-ins, sign out on shared computers |
| **Physical controls** | Property and records are protected | The lock walk, the vacant unit check, the key log, the control key signed out |
| **Record retention** | The evidence exists when it is needed | Photos on tickets, receipts on claims, incident forms the same day |

Controls feel like friction on a busy day. They are the reason a storage company can take in rent from a thousand tenants through five sites and know, to the dollar, that it all arrived.

## The Records

An auditor, an insurer, a lender or a tax examiner will one day ask for the records behind a number. What must exist:

- **Every revenue transaction** in Cubby, with its tenant, unit and date, and the batch that took it to the bank.
- **Every waiver, credit and refund** with its reason and who approved it.
- **Every vendor invoice** with its ticket or PO, scope, approval, inspection and payment.
- **Every card charge** with its receipt and coding.
- **Every payroll period** with the signed Time Sheets behind it.
- **Every capital asset** with its invoice, in-service date and, in Asset Management, its warranty and permit.
- **Every lien sale** with its notices, its advertisement, its sale record and its proceeds.
- **Every incident** with its form, photos and the claim correspondence.

Retention periods are set by law and policy and are long; nothing in this list is ever "cleaned up." Nexus keeps most of it by design (tickets, forms, Asset Management records are never deleted by code), and Cubby keeps the tenant side.

## The Audit

An **audit** is an independent check that the statements fairly present what happened. The auditor does not re-add every number; they test. They pick transactions and follow the trail (a rent payment to the bank, an invoice to the ticket to the photo), they confirm balances with outsiders (the bank, the lender), they test the controls (did the approvals happen, did the reconciliations), and they look hardest at the judgments: the allowance, the capitalization calls, the accruals.

For a site, an audit is a request for records that already exist. A site whose tickets carry their photos, whose invoices carry their numbers and whose waivers carry their reasons has nothing to prepare. One that does not spends the audit looking for paper.

> [!TIP]
> The test for every record you make is the same: could a stranger, a year from now, understand what happened and why from this alone? If yes, it will pass any audit. If no, add what is missing today.

## A Greens Scenario

The auditor picks a repairs invoice from Temecula's ledger: $640 plus parts, the gate vendor. Accounting pulls the payable: the invoice carries "#4830". The ticket shows the scope, the quote, Dana's approval, the finished-work photo and Resolved. The vendor's Compliance tab shows a current certificate on the day of the visit. The auditor moves to the next sample. Nobody at the site is called.

## Common Mistakes

- **A budget line that is last year plus a percent.**
- **A variance comment with no ticket number** and no "timing or real."
- **Controls skipped on a busy day.** That is what they are for.
- **Records "cleaned up."** Retention is long, and Nexus and Cubby keep them for a reason.

## Key Points

- A budget is next year's income statement, built from the rent roll and the schedules; a budget-to-actual report is read through its variance comments.
- A good comment says what moved, why, timing or real, and what next, from the drill-down with the ticket numbers.
- Internal controls (segregated duties, approval limits, matching, no cash outside the system, reasons on waivers, reconciliations, access, physical controls, retention) each ask something of the site.
- An audit follows trails and tests controls; a site with complete records has nothing to prepare.
