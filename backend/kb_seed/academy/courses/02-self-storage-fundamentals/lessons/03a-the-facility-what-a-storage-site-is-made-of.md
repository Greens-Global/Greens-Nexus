## The Gate Stops Halfway

On your morning walk at Fairfield, the gate opens on your code, stops halfway and reverses. Is it the track, the sensor or the motor, and is it yours to fix? This lesson walks a storage site from the street in, so you know what every part does and what to do when it fails.

## From the Street In

Every site is built for three jobs: keep out people who do not rent, let people who do rent in without staff, and let a loaded truck reach every door.

## The Perimeter

A site is closed on every side: chain-link (often with privacy slats), wrought iron, or a concrete block wall, and often the back wall of a building. On your walks, look for a cut or peeled-back corner of fence, a gap washed out underneath, or pallets stacked outside like a ladder. Each one is a way in. Photograph it and report it.

## The Gate and Keypads

The gate moves hundreds of times a day, so it breaks more than anything else on the site.

- **The gate** slides on a track or hangs from rollers (cantilever), or swings on hinges; a motor, a chain and a controller move it.
- **The entry keypad** stands at driver's-window height. The tenant keys their code, the controller checks it, the gate opens. Many have an intercom to the office.
- **The exit** is a second keypad (which logs who left) or an **exit loop**: a wire loop under the pavement that senses a vehicle and opens the gate with no code.
- **Safety loops and photo eyes** stop the gate from closing on a vehicle still in the opening.
- **Stacking room** is the paved length where a car with a trailer waits without blocking the road; the **hold-open time** must suit a long rig.
- A **manual release** for power failures and a **fire department access** method (a key box or a code) exist at every gate. Your manager knows where. Never give the fire code to anyone.

A stone in the track is yours to clear. A motor, sensor or controller fault is not: put the gate on manual with your manager and the gate company is called.

> [!TIP]
> "The gate would not open": ask what code they entered, what time it was and what the keypad showed. The answer is usually the hours, a typo or a suspended account.

## The Office and the Aisles

The office sits where staff can **see the gate**: you see who arrives, and arrivals see you. It holds the counter, the retail wall, the camera monitor, the facility's keys and overlocks, and the only private tenant records on site.

Aisles are built for moving trucks. **Fire lanes** are painted and signed and are never blocked, not by a trailer "for an hour," not by a dumpster. **Bollards** (steel crash posts) at corners and keypads take the hits that would take out a wall; a bent one did its job, so report it for replacement. Walking speed, always.

## The Buildings

| Building | What it is | For the tenant |
|---|---|---|
| **Drive-up row** | One story, roll-up doors facing the aisle | Park at the door; dust and heat reach the unit |
| **Hallway building** | Doors off an inside corridor | More protected; a cart from truck to unit |
| **Multi-story** | Usually climate-controlled, with a loading bay, elevators and carts | Most protection, often cheapest upstairs, longest trip |

Hallway lights are often on motion sensors, which is why a hall looks dark until you step in.

## Lights, Cameras, Signs

Lights on the eaves and poles run dusk to dawn or on timers; a dark corner is where break-ins and falls happen, so report a dead light the day you see it. **Cameras** record the gate, office, aisles and halls and overwrite after a set number of days; they are a record, not a guard. **Signs** state the rules at the gate, mark buildings and unit ranges, and post the **clear height** at covered rows and enclosed doors.

## Water and Fire

Roofs leak at seams, gutters clog, and a lot that drains toward a row sends water at the doors. After a storm, walk the rows and look at the low corners of drive-up doors: a dark stain at the base means water got in, and the tenant should hear it from you first. Fire protection is extinguishers on tagged inspections, sprinklers in most multi-story buildings, and clear fire lanes and hydrants.

## Common Mistakes

- **Letting a tenant park in a fire lane** "for a minute."
- **Fixing the gate motor or controller** yourself.
- **Walking past** a dead light, a bent bollard or a cut fence.
- **Not knowing your own gate**: the exit method, the manual release, the hold-open time.

## Key Points

- A site is a closed perimeter, one gate, an office that watches it, aisles that reach every door and three kinds of building.
- The gate has an entry keypad, an exit keypad or loop, safety loops, stacking room and a manual release; it breaks most.
- Fire lanes are never blocked; bollards are meant to be hit.
- Report every broken light, fence, drain and bollard the day you see it.
