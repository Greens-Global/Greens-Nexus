## What You'll Be Able to Do

- Name the parts of a storage site from the street to the back fence, and say what each one is for.
- Explain how the gate, the keypads and the exit loop work together.
- Tell a drive-up building from a hallway building and a multi-story building, and what that means for the tenant.
- Describe what a unit is made of: the slab, the steel partitions, the mesh above them and the roll-up door.
- Name the parts of a roll-up door and the latch where the lock goes.
- Spot the things that break (gates, doors, lights, posts, drains) and know where to report them.

## A Storage Site From the Street In

Every storage site is built the same way for the same reasons: keep people who do not rent out, let people who do rent in without staff, and let a loaded truck reach every door. Walk it in this order and you will know any site in the country.

| Part | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Perimeter fence or wall** | Keeps the property closed except at the gate |
| **Gate and keypads** | Lets a tenant in with their own code and logs it |
| **Office** | Rentals, payments, the retail wall, the cameras and the keys |
| **Drive aisles** | Get a truck to every door and a fire engine to every building |
| **Buildings** | Rows of drive-up units, hallway buildings, multi-story climate buildings |
| **Units** | The thing the tenant rents: a slab, steel walls, a door and the tenant's lock |
| **Lighting, cameras, signs** | Make the site safe to use and easy to find your way around |
| **Drainage and fire protection** | Keep water out of units and fire from spreading |
| **The vehicle lot** | Parking spaces for RVs, boats and trailers (the RV & Boat Storage course) |

## The Perimeter: Fences and Walls

```figure
{
  "kind": "drawing",
  "title": "Chain-Link With Slats, or a Block Wall",
  "draw": "facility-fence",
  "label": "The two common perimeters",
  "note": "Chain-link is cheap and sees through; privacy slats block the view. A block wall is quieter, stronger and hides the lot. Cameras usually sit on the posts and corners."
}
```

A storage site is closed on every side. The common choices are **chain-link fence**, often with **privacy slats** woven through it so passers-by cannot see what is stored, **wrought iron or tube steel** where the site wants to look open, and **concrete block walls**, which are the strongest and the quietest and are common in California. Many sites use the back of a building as part of the perimeter: the units' rear wall is the property line.

What to notice on your walks: a cut or peeled-back corner of chain-link, a missing slat, a gap under the fence where the ground washed out, a tree or a stack of pallets outside that makes a ladder, and a wall with graffiti. Each one is a way in or a sign that someone is looking for one. Photograph it and report it.

## The Gate and the Keypads

```figure
{
  "kind": "drawing",
  "title": "The Gate",
  "draw": "facility-gate",
  "label": "A sliding gate, the keypad pedestal and the exit loop",
  "note": "The keypad stands where a driver reaches it from the window. The dashed loop in the pavement senses a vehicle leaving so the gate opens without a code."
}
```

The gate is the one opening in the perimeter, and it is the part of the site that breaks most often, because it moves hundreds of times a day.

- **The gate itself** is usually a **sliding gate** on a track or a cantilever (it hangs from rollers and never touches the ground), or a **swing gate** on hinges. Sliding gates take less room. A motor, a chain and a controller move it.
- **The entry keypad** stands on a pedestal at the driver's window height, far enough back from the gate that the car is not in the gate's path. The tenant keys their code, the controller checks it against the management system, and the gate opens. Many keypads have an intercom button to the office.
- **The exit** is either a second keypad (so the system logs who left and when) or an **exit loop**: a wire loop under the pavement that senses a vehicle and opens the gate from the inside. A loop is convenient; a keypad on the way out gives a complete access log. Know which your site has.
- **Safety loops and photo eyes** stop the gate from closing on a vehicle that is still in the opening. A gate that closes on a trailer is a damage claim.
- **Stacking room** is the paved length between the street and the gate where a car, or a truck with a trailer, waits without blocking the sidewalk or the road.
- **A pedestrian gate** with its own keypad lets a tenant walk in. On some sites it is the only after-hours entry.
- **The hold-open time** is how long the gate stays open after a code. A long rig needs longer than a car (the RV & Boat Storage course comes back to this).

> [!TIP]
> When a tenant says "the gate would not open," ask three questions: What code did you enter? What time was it? What did the keypad show? The answer is usually the hours, a mistyped code or a suspended account, and the access log settles it. The Access, Locks and Security lesson covers codes and the log.

The gate also has a **manual release** for a power failure and a **fire department access** method (a Knox box with the gate key, or a code the fire department holds). Your manager knows where they are. Never give the fire department code to anyone else.

## The Office

The office is placed where staff can **see the gate** and the entry aisle. That sight line is part of the site's security and part of its sales: you see who arrives, and arrivals see you.

Inside you will usually find the counter and the management system, the **retail wall** (locks, boxes, tape, mattress covers), the camera monitor, the keys to the facility's own locks, the overlocks, a restroom tenants may ask to use, and the paperwork that still lives on paper. Many older sites have a **manager's apartment** attached. Some newer ones have a **kiosk** or a lockbox for after-hours rentals.

The office is the one place on the site that holds a tenant's private information. The Privacy and Compliance lesson tells you how to keep it.

## Drive Aisles and Traffic

The aisles between buildings are built for moving trucks. Between two rows of drive-up doors an aisle is commonly in the range of 25 to 30 ft, wide enough for a truck to park at a door while another vehicle passes. Vehicle lots need far more, because a 40 ft rig has to swing into its space.

What to know about aisles:

- **Fire lanes.** Painted curbs and signs mark the lanes a fire engine needs. Nothing is parked in them, not a tenant's trailer "just for an hour," not a dumpster, not a delivery.
- **One-way aisles.** Some sites run traffic one way around a building. Faded arrows cause head-on standoffs between two trucks.
- **Speed.** Aisles are full of open doors, people carrying things and children. Walking speed, always.
- **Bollards (crash posts)** stand at building corners, at canopy posts and beside keypads. They take the hits that would otherwise take out a wall. A bent bollard did its job; report it so it is replaced.
- **Downspouts** at building corners are the other thing trucks hit.

Most storage buildings are **steel**: a frame, a metal roof and metal walls, on a concrete slab. Older ones are concrete block. They come in three shapes.

| Building | What it is | What it means for the tenant |
|---|---|---|
| **Drive-up row** | A long single-story building with roll-up doors on one or both long sides, facing the aisle | Park at the door, no walk, no stairs; dust and outside temperatures reach the unit |
| **Hallway building** | Units open onto an inside corridor, usually with a roll-up or swing door at each end of the hall | More protected from weather and dust; a cart from the truck to the unit |
| **Multi-story** | Two or more floors, usually climate-controlled, with a **loading bay** or covered drop-off, **freight elevators** and **carts** | The most protection and often the lowest price per square foot upstairs; the longest trip from the truck |

Two more things you will see on buildings: **unit numbers** painted or on plates above each door, in a system that tells you the building and the row (your site's numbering is on its own page), and **hallway lights on motion sensors** in interior buildings, which is why a hall looks dark until you step in.

## Inside a Unit

```figure
{
  "kind": "drawing",
  "title": "A Unit Is Walls, Not a Room",
  "draw": "unit-mesh-ceiling",
  "label": "Steel partitions, mesh above them, the building's own roof far above",
  "note": "The partitions stop short of the roof. Mesh closes the gap so nobody climbs over and so sprinklers, air and light reach every unit."
}
```

A unit is simpler than it looks from the outside.

- **The floor** is the building's concrete slab. A drive-up unit's slab sits a little above the aisle, with a small **rain lip** at the door, so water running down the aisle does not run in. Water that does get in finds the low corner.
- **The walls** are **steel partitions**: corrugated or flat galvanized panels bolted to posts. They are not load-bearing and they are not soundproof. A tenant can hear a neighbor.
- **There is no ceiling of its own.** Partitions stop a foot or more below the roof, and the gap is closed with **wire mesh**. The mesh keeps anyone from climbing from one unit into the next, and it lets the building's sprinklers, air handling and lighting serve every unit. In a climate-controlled building the air that is conditioned is the building's air, which reaches the unit through that gap. That is also why a strong smell, smoke or pests in one unit reach its neighbors.
- **There is usually no light, no outlet and no water** inside a unit. Lights are in the hallway or on the building's eave. A tenant who runs an extension cord to a unit is breaking the lease and starting a fire risk.
- **Climate-controlled buildings** are insulated and sealed, with heating and cooling for the building as a whole. "Climate-controlled" describes the building, not a thermostat in the unit (the Product lesson tells you how to say it honestly).

## The Roll-Up Door

```figure
{
  "kind": "drawing",
  "title": "The Parts of a Roll-Up Door",
  "draw": "door-roll-up",
  "label": "Hood, guides, curtain, bottom bar, handle, latch",
  "note": "The curtain rolls up onto a drum inside the hood. Springs in the drum carry its weight. The latch at the bottom corner is where the lock goes."
}
```

The roll-up door is the part of a unit a tenant touches every visit, and the part staff fix most. Learn its parts.

| Part | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Hood and drum** | The box above the opening. Inside it the curtain rolls onto a drum, and **springs** inside the drum counterbalance the door's weight so it lifts with one hand |
| **Guides** | The vertical tracks on each side that the curtain rides in |
| **Curtain** | The corrugated steel sheet that is the door |
| **Bottom bar** | The stiff bar along the bottom edge, with a **handle** and a weather seal |
| **Latch** | A steel plate and slide bolt at the bottom corner. The bolt slides into a hole in the guide so the door cannot be lifted, and the latch carries the hole, or two holes, the lock goes through. The Access, Locks and Security lesson looks at the latch, the hasp and every kind of lock up close |

How a door is used well, and what goes wrong:

- **Lift from the handle, in the middle.** Lifting from one corner twists the curtain in the guides and that is how a door starts to bind.
- **A door that is heavy or will not stay up** has lost spring tension. Adjusting a spring is a job for a trained person with the right bar; a spring under tension can break a wrist. Report it, do not fix it.
- **A door that will not close all the way** usually has something in a guide or a bent bottom bar. Look before you force it. Never force a door.
- **A door that opens on its own** when unlatched is over-tensioned, and will slam up with a tenant's hand on it. Report it.
- **The latch must slide freely.** A latch that needs a kick is a latch a tenant will leave undone.
- **Lubrication** of the guides and the springs is routine maintenance on a schedule, not a can of spray in a tenant's hand.

> [!WARNING]
> A unit door that is open with nobody in it, a door that is unlatched with a lock hanging open, or a door that is damaged, is a security event. Secure it, photograph it and tell your manager. The Running the Site: Open to Close lesson has the routine.

## Lighting, Cameras and Signs

- **Lighting** is on the eaves of every building and on poles over the aisles and the lot, usually on photocells (dusk to dawn) or timers that match access hours. A dark corner is where a break-in happens and where a tenant trips. Report every dead light the day you see it.
- **Cameras** watch the gate, the office, the aisles and the hallways. They are recorded, and the recording is overwritten after a set number of days unless someone saves it. They are a record, not a guard; nobody is watching live. The Access, Locks and Security lesson covers footage and the Privacy and Compliance lesson covers who may see it.
- **Signs** tell tenants the rules at the gate (hours, no living on site, no hazardous materials), tell them where they are (building letters, unit number ranges at the end of each aisle, "You Are Here" maps on bigger sites), and tell drivers what fits (the **clear height** at every covered row and enclosed door, which the RV & Boat Storage course insists on). A missing or faded sign is a maintenance item, and in a dispute a sign that was there matters.

## Water, Drainage and Fire Protection

Water is the quiet enemy of a storage building. Roofs leak at seams and penetrations, gutters clog, and a lot that drains toward a row of doors sends a sheet of water at the rain lips in a storm.

- **Gutters and downspouts** run along the eaves and down the corners. A downspout that drains onto the aisle in front of a door, instead of into a drain, soaks that unit.
- **Drains, swales and the slope of the lot** carry water to the street or a retention basin. Standing water in an aisle after rain is a report.
- After any storm, **walk the rows and look at the low corners** of drive-up units from outside: a dark stain at the base of a door means water got in. The tenant should hear it from you before they find it themselves.

Fire protection is what the fire code requires for the building type:

- **Fire extinguishers** in the office, in hallways and at set spacing along drive-up rows, inspected on a tag.
- **Sprinklers** in most multi-story and climate-controlled buildings, which is one reason the mesh gap above partitions exists.
- **Fire lanes and hydrants** kept clear, and building addresses visible from the street.
- **No smoking, no open flame, no fuel, no propane** in units. The lease forbids them and the Safety and Emergencies lesson tells you what to do when you find them.

## The Vehicle Lot and the Mailbox Room

Behind or beside the buildings, most Greens sites have a **vehicle lot**: open spaces on gravel or pavement, covered spaces under a canopy at Temecula, and two enclosed garages at Valley Center (From Greens' Cubby, 11/2026). The lot has its own rules for aisles, turning room, posts and clear heights, and the RV & Boat Storage course spends a whole lesson on it.

Escondido and Valley Center also have a **mailbox room**: a wall of locking boxes with their own keys, a package shelf behind the counter, and the rules the Postal Service sets for a commercial mail receiving agency (From Greens' Cubby, 11/2026, for the mailbox counts; the Product lesson has them).

## Walk Your Site: Find Each Thing

Before the Hands-On Check, walk your own site once with this list and find every item. Where your site does not have one, write "none" so you know.

- [ ] The perimeter: what kind of fence or wall, and where a building is the fence.
- [ ] The gate: sliding or swing, the entry keypad, the exit (keypad or loop), the pedestrian gate, the manual release, the fire department access.
- [ ] The office sight line to the gate, the retail wall, where the facility keys and overlocks are kept.
- [ ] The fire lanes, the one-way arrows, the bollards.
- [ ] Each building: drive-up row, hallway building or multi-story, and how the unit numbers run.
- [ ] One unit of each kind, open and vacant: the slab, the partitions, the mesh, the door and its latch.
- [ ] The lights and their switches or timers, the cameras and the monitor.
- [ ] The drains and downspouts, the low spots after rain.
- [ ] The extinguishers and their tags, the hydrants, the sprinkler riser room if there is one.
- [ ] The vehicle lot and, at Escondido and Valley Center, the mailbox room.

## Greens Scenarios

**Escondido.** A tenant on the second floor of the climate building says her unit "has no ceiling, anyone could climb in." You explain the mesh: nobody can get through it, and it is there so the sprinklers and the conditioned air reach her unit. She is reassured, and you note that the question comes up often enough to belong on the tour.

**Fairfield.** On the morning walk the gate opens on your code but stops halfway and reverses. You check the track for gravel, find a stone from the lot in it, clear it, and watch it cycle twice. If it had been the motor or the sensor, you would have left it, put the gate on manual with your manager and called the gate company.

**Murrieta.** A tenant lifting his door by one corner has twisted the curtain and it is stuck a foot up. You do not force it. You ask him to stop, photograph it, mark the unit in the system and raise a ticket for the door company, and you offer him access to his things once it is freed.

**Temecula.** After the first rain of the season you walk the drive-up rows and see a stain at the base of three doors in the lowest row. You photograph each, check the downspout above them (it is disconnected), report it, and your manager calls the three tenants that morning.

**A Texas site.** A tenant asks why the hallway "goes dark." You explain the motion sensors save power and come on as he walks, and you check that every section actually comes on. One does not; you report the sensor.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forcing a door** that is binding, or trying to adjust a spring.
- **Letting a tenant park in a fire lane** "for a minute."
- **Walking past a dead light, a bent bollard or a cut fence** because it is not your job. Reporting it is.
- **Promising a unit has its own ceiling, its own light or its own temperature.** It has walls, mesh and a door.
- **Not knowing your own gate**: whether the exit logs, where the manual release is, how long the hold-open runs.

## Key Points

- A site is a closed perimeter, one gate, an office that watches it, aisles that reach every door, and buildings of three kinds: drive-up rows, hallway buildings and multi-story.
- The gate is a sliding or swing gate with an entry keypad, an exit keypad or loop, safety loops, stacking room and a hold-open time; it is the thing that breaks most.
- A unit is the slab, steel partitions, wire mesh in the gap below the roof, and a roll-up door with a latch for the lock. No ceiling of its own, no light, no outlet.
- A roll-up door is a hood and drum with springs, guides, a curtain, a bottom bar with a handle, and a latch. Lift from the middle; never force it; springs are a technician's job.
- Lights, cameras, signs, drains and extinguishers are the site's safety; report what is broken the day you see it.
- Walk your site and find each thing before the Hands-On Check.
