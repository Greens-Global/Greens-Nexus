## "Can You Keep an Eye on My Stuff?"

A new Escondido tenant asks you to hold a spare key "just in case." At Temecula a man says he is a tenant's brother and needs the gate code. Both requests sound reasonable and both are a firm no. This lesson teaches the rule behind every answer about access: the tenant alone controls the unit.

## Exclusive Control

The tenant supplies their own lock and keeps the only key. Greens does not hold a key, does not go into a rented unit and does not know what is inside. That is why the rental agreement says it creates **no bailment**: a bailment is when someone takes custody of your goods, like a valet with your car keys, and becomes responsible for them. Greens rents space; it never takes custody. That is a big reason the tenant needs their own protection (see the lesson Tenant Protection and Insurance).

So you never ask for or keep a copy of a tenant's key, never go into a rented unit "to check," never ask what is inside, and never open a unit for anyone who is not the tenant or an authorized person on the account.

> [!TIP]
> "We watch the property with cameras and gate controls, and only you have the key to your unit. That's why we recommend protection for your belongings."

## Gate Codes and the Access Log

Every tenant has their own gate code, kept on their lease in Cubby.

- One code per tenant, never shared or reused: the log ties each entry to one account.
- A gate code is a password. Never read one to an unverified caller or write one where others can see.
- Never let a tenant or vendor in on your own code; your code is logged as you.
- When staff leave, the codes they knew are changed.

The gate records each code use. At Greens only Fairfield's gate reports into Cubby today, where each try shows as allowed, master code, unknown code or outside of hours. The log is evidence after a break-in or a dispute; never edit or share it outside the process (see the lesson Privacy and Compliance).

**Gate hours** (when tenants can get in) are longer than **office hours** (when staff are there). Customers mix them up, so tell every new tenant both. Each site's hours are on Greens Site Hours and Access. A code refused outside gate hours is not a broken code.

A delinquent tenant's gate code is changed by Cubby at a set step of the ladder; the steps are taught in the lesson Delinquency and the Lien Process.

## Which Lock to Recommend

| Lock | Strength |
|---|---|
| **Disc lock** | A round body that hides the shackle; bolt cutters cannot get a grip. The standard recommendation |
| **Cylinder lock** | A barrel that sits inside the latch; nothing exposed at all. Works only with a latch made for it |
| **Padlock** | An exposed shackle; easy to cut. The weakest |

"Most tenants use a disc lock. The shackle is covered, so bolt cutters can't get a grip. We sell them here." It is honest, it helps the tenant and it is a sale. How the latch, hasp and cylinder core work is in the lesson Locks Up Close: Hasp, Latch and Cylinder.

## Overlocks

An **overlock** is the facility's own lock, added beside the tenant's (never in place of it) so the door cannot open. It goes on:

- a **delinquent** unit, only when Cubby flags Overlock Needed;
- a **vacant** unit, so nobody moves in without renting;
- a rented unit **found unlocked**, until the tenant comes to put their own lock on.

The rules: record every overlock in Cubby the same day; check the unit number twice; never overlock a paid-up unit; remove it promptly when the tenant pays and check their gate code works; the tenant never removes an overlock. A paid tenant who finds an overlock is one of the industry's top complaints.

## Cutting a Lock

A tenant's lock is cut only under an authorized, documented process: a step in the lien process that allows it, a court order or search warrant, a tenant who lost their key, proves who they are and signs an authorization (a fee applies), or a real emergency handled by the manager. Every cut has the authorization, a witness, photos before and after, an inventory where the process requires one, and a new facility lock. Your manager decides; you never cut a lock on your own.

## Cameras

Cameras cover the gate, office, aisles and halls. After any incident, tell your manager right away so the footage is **saved** before the system overwrites it. Never show footage to the public, another tenant or a customer; police get it through legal process.

## At Greens

**Escondido.** Unit 1142 is closed with no lock; Cubby shows it rented and paid. You put a facility lock on, note it in Cubby and call the tenant. You do not open the door to look.

**Valley Center.** A tenant pays in full at 10:00 AM. You take her overlock off that morning, record it, and check her gate code before she leaves.

**Temecula.** The "brother" gets no code, and no confirmation that anyone rents at Greens.

## Common Mistakes

- **Keeping a spare key** "to help."
- **Reading a gate code** to someone you have not verified.
- **Leaving an overlock on** after payment, or putting one on the wrong unit.
- **Letting footage be overwritten** after an incident.

## Key Points

- The tenant has exclusive control and the only key; storage creates no bailment.
- One gate code per tenant; codes are passwords; the access log is evidence.
- Tell new tenants both gate hours and office hours.
- Recommend a disc lock; padlocks are the weakest.
- Overlocks only where Cubby flags them, recorded, never on a paid unit, off promptly when paid.
- A lock is cut only with authorization, a witness and photos.
