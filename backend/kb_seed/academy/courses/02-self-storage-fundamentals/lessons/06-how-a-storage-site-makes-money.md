## "Why Does My Neighbor Pay Less?"

A Temecula tenant calls, annoyed: his rent went up, and he heard the space next to his rents for less. On the same day your manager says the lot is "full but not earning." Both make sense once you know how a site makes money. This lesson teaches the rates, the three kinds of occupancy, and the late-fee math you will check on ledgers every month.

## Where the Money Comes From

| Source | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Rent** | Units, parking spaces, mailboxes. By far the largest |
| **Fees** | Admin fee, late fee, lien and auction fees |
| **Protection** | Monthly protection plan charges |
| **Merchandise** | Locks, boxes, tape, covers |

Everything beyond rent is **ancillary income**.

## Rates

- **Street rate:** today's price for a new customer for that unit type. It moves with how full that size is, the season and competitors. Some operators price lower online than at the counter; always quote what Cubby shows for that channel.
- **In-place rate:** what an existing tenant pays now. Two identical units side by side can pay different rents. That is normal, and never something to discuss between customers.
- **Rate increase on an existing tenant (ECRI):** storage is month-to-month, so rent can change with the notice the agreement requires. Moving costs a tenant a truck and a day, so many accept a modest increase; too large or too frequent, and they leave and post reviews.

What you do: never promise "your rate will never go up" (in California, newer agreements show the 12-month maximum on page one; see the lesson The Rental Agreement). Explain calmly that the increase came with notice, offer options such as a smaller or upstairs unit, and send a request for an exception to your manager. Greens sets rates centrally, not at the counter.

## Occupancy: Three Kinds

| Kind | Formula | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Unit occupancy** | Units rented / total units | How many doors are rented |
| **Physical occupancy** | Rented square feet / total rentable square feet | How much space is rented |
| **Economic occupancy** | Rent achieved / gross potential rent | How much of the possible money the site earns |

**Gross potential rent** is what the site would earn with every unit rented at today's street rate.

**Units versus square feet.** A site has 200 units and 100 are rented: 50% unit occupancy. If the rented ones are the big units and add up to 80% of the square feet, physical occupancy is 80%.

**Economic occupancy.** Ten 5x10s at a street rate of $50: gross potential is $500. All ten are rented, but one pays an older $45 and one was given free to a charity. Rent achieved is 8 x $50 + $45 + $0 = $445, so economic occupancy is $445 / $500 = 89%, while unit occupancy is 100%. Discounts, free units and unpaid rent all pull economic occupancy down. A tenant who does not pay still counts as "rented," which is why the collection calls early in the month protect revenue.

> [!TIP]
> Full is not the goal. Revenue is. A site at 95% with heavy discounts and unpaid rent can earn less than one at 88% where everyone pays a fair rate.

## Concessions

A **concession** is a move-in promotion: a free first month, $1 for the first month, half off for two months. It brings in move-ins and lowers economic occupancy while it lasts. In California a promotional rate and its length must be on page one of the agreement. Offer only what Cubby shows; never invent one.

## Fees and Late-Fee Math

Greens charges a one-time admin fee at move-in, a late fee when rent goes unpaid, and lien and sale fees only when those steps really happen. Every amount is on Greens Fees and Charges. Never add a fee that is not in the agreement and on that page.

California caps the late fee by monthly rent, and Greens' late fee is set at that cap:

| Monthly rent | Late fee |
|---|---|
| $60 or less | $10 |
| Over $60 and under $100 | $15 |
| $100 or more | The greater of $20 or 15% of rent |

Work it every time the same way: find the band, then for $100 or more compare 15% with $20.

- **$180:** 15% is $27, more than $20, so **$27**.
- **$120:** 15% is $18; $20 is greater, so **$20**.
- **$100:** 15% is $15; $20 is greater, so **$20**.
- **$75:** over $60 and under $100, so **$15**.
- **$50:** $60 or less, so **$10**.

The other California rules: no late fee until rent is at least 10 days unpaid, only if the agreement states it, and only one per missed payment. Cubby posts the late fee by itself on the day the ladder sets (see the lesson Delinquency and the Lien Process); you never type one by hand. Your job is to recognize the right amount on a ledger.

## Merchandise

A disc lock at move-in is revenue and security at once. "Do you have a lock, or would you like one of ours?" and "Do you have enough boxes and tape?" are offers, not pressure.

## Your Habits Are Revenue

| Habit | What it moves |
|---|---|
| Answer every call, follow up every lead | Move-ins |
| Quote Cubby's current rate | No lost money from made-up discounts |
| Size customers well | Fewer early move-outs |
| Offer protection, a lock, supplies and autopay | Ancillary income, fewer late payments |
| Make the early collection calls | Economic occupancy |
| Keep unit status true on your walk | No unit lost as "occupied" when it is empty |

## Common Mistakes

- **Making up a discount** to close a sale.
- **Comparing two tenants' rates** with either of them.
- **Thinking full means successful.**
- **Missing the band edge:** at exactly $100 the late fee is $20, not $15.

## Key Points

- Street rate is today's new-customer price; in-place rate is what a tenant pays now.
- Never promise a rate will not change; send exceptions to your manager.
- Unit occupancy counts doors, physical counts square feet, economic counts money earned.
- California late fee: $10 up to $60, $15 over $60 and under $100, the greater of $20 or 15% from $100.
- Offer protection, a lock, supplies and autopay at every move-in.
