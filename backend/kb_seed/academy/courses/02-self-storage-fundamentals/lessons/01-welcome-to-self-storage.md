## Where Self-Storage Came From

Modern self-storage grew up in Texas in the 1960s. Most histories point to a small cinder-block-and-steel building in Odessa, Texas, built by Russ Williams and his stepson Bob Munn and marketed to oil-field companies that needed a place for tools and pipe. The idea was simple: the customer brings their own lock, keeps the only key, and comes and goes on their own. Similar buildings soon opened in Midland and Brownsville.

The idea reached California quickly. **Public Storage** was founded on 08/14/1972 in **El Cajon, California**, in San Diego County, not far from Greens' Escondido and Valley Center sites. B. Wayne Hughes and Kenneth Volk Jr. started it with about $50,000 after seeing how well mini-storage was doing around Dallas and Houston. Public Storage became one of the largest real estate companies in the country, with 3,073 owned facilities plus 307 managed for others (as of 12/31/2024).

Three things drove the growth over the next fifty years:

1. **People own more things and move more often.** Homes, apartments and garages fill up.
2. **Life events.** Deaths, divorces, downsizing and moves create sudden storage needs (the Why People Rent lesson covers these).
3. **Business use.** Contractors, sales reps and online sellers found storage cheaper than warehouse space.

Reality TV made storage auctions famous. As you will learn in the Delinquency and the Lien Process lesson and the Lines You Never Cross lesson, real lien sales are a strict legal process and a last resort, not entertainment.

## How Big the Industry Is

Numbers about self-storage disagree from source to source, so this course gives one dated source for each and asks you to use the rounded version.

| Measure | Figure | Source and date |
|---|---|---|
| U.S. households that rent storage | About 12.6%, roughly 1 in 8 | Self Storage Association Demand Study, 2025 (households in 2024) |
| U.S. storage facilities | More than 50,000 | California Senate Judiciary Committee analysis, 07/2025 |
| California storage facilities | About 3,500 | California Senate Judiciary Committee analysis, 07/2025 |
| National occupancy | About 85.5% | TractIQ, 08/2026 |
| Yearly industry revenue | Tens of billions of dollars | Sources disagree widely; use the words, not a number |

The rule of thumb to remember: **about 1 in 8 U.S. households rents storage.** That share has grown over time. It is why there is a storage site in almost every town and why competition is real. When national occupancy drops from its pandemic peak, as it has, every caller matters more and every tenant you keep matters more.

> [!TIP]
> When a customer asks "Is storage a big business?", you do not need a statistic. "About one in eight households rents storage" is accurate, dated and easy to say.

## Who Owns and Runs Storage

Three kinds of companies run storage sites. You will meet all three as competitors, and you may meet the third one inside Greens.

### REITs

A **REIT** (Real Estate Investment Trust) is a publicly traded company that owns real estate. The large storage REITs include Public Storage, Extra Space Storage and CubeSmart, and U-Haul and National Storage Affiliates are also among the top operators (Inside Self-Storage top operators list, 2025). REITs have big marketing budgets, call centers and revenue management teams. Their sites are often large, multi-story buildings.

### Independents

**Independents** are everyone else who owns and runs their own sites: single-site owners (often called mom-and-pop) and private regional operators with a handful to a few dozen sites. The top 100 operators hold about 63% of rentable square feet but only about 34% of facilities (Self Storage Almanac, as cited in a 2025 annual report). In plain words: the big companies own the big buildings, and most facilities in the country are still independent.

**Greens is a private regional operator.** That means decisions are close to the sites, policies are Greens' own, and every employee's work shows up directly in how a site does.

### Third-Party Management

In **third-party management**, an owner hires a management company to run the site under the manager's brand, systems, pricing and staff. The owner keeps the building; the manager takes a fee. Several REITs run large third-party management businesses.

Three questions sort out who does what at any site:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Who owns the building? | The owner sets the big decisions and receives the income |
| Who employs the staff? | Your employer's policies and training apply to you |
| Whose rental agreement and lien process apply? | That agreement is what the tenant signed, so it controls fees, notices and the lien |

> [!NOTE]
> A site that is managed by a third party follows its manager's procedures. If you work with one, ask your manager which procedures apply before you act on anything in this course.

## Where Greens Fits

Greens Storage runs five sites in California today, all on Cubby: **Escondido, Fairfield, Murrieta, Temecula and Valley Center**. Texas sites are coming, and when they join, this course already teaches the Texas rules beside the California ones.

Greens rents three kinds of space. Here is what is active in Cubby today (From Greens' Cubby, 11/2026):

| Kind of space | Active units | Leased | Where most of them are |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enclosed storage units | 1,331 | 1,194 | Escondido (717) and Valley Center (568) |
| Vehicle parking spaces | 1,038 | 777 | Temecula (481), Fairfield (303), Murrieta (161) |
| Mailboxes | 356 | 71 | Valley Center (279) and Escondido (77) |

Two things stand out:

1. **Vehicle parking is a large share of Greens' business.** Fairfield, Murrieta and Temecula are almost all parking: RVs, boats, trailers and cars. That is why the next course, RV & Boat Storage Fundamentals, comes right after this one.
2. **Each site is different.** Escondido and Valley Center are mostly classic storage units. Fairfield has 303 parking spaces and only 4 enclosed units. Valley Center also rents mailboxes. When you start at a site, learn its mix first.

Every Greens site bills on the first of the month (From Greens' Cubby, 11/2026). You will see why that matters in the How a Storage Site Makes Money lesson and in the lien lessons.

### A Day in the Life

Here is what a normal day can look like at a Greens site:

- At Escondido, you open the office, check overnight gate activity, take three calls about unit sizes, rent a 10x10 to a family that is moving, and walk the hallways to check locks.
- At Temecula, you answer a question about whether a 35-foot fifth-wheel trailer fits a covered space, take a payment from a boat owner, and walk the lot to check that every space holds the vehicle on its lease.
- At Valley Center, you hand a mailbox key to a new mailbox customer, sell a disc lock and a box kit, and call two tenants whose autopay failed.
- At a Texas site, once it joins, the work is the same, but some legal steps and notices differ. You will learn both sets of rules side by side.

A few words you will see in every lesson. The full glossary is in the Numbers That Matter and Words to Know lesson.

|---|---|

## Common Mistakes New Hires Make

- **Treating storage as "just real estate."** Most renters are in the middle of a life change. Service drives reviews, referrals and how long people stay.
- **Repeating TV myths.** Auctions are not treasure hunts, and staff never "keep the good stuff."
- **Assuming every site is the same.** Learn your site's mix of units, parking and mailboxes.
- **Guessing at numbers.** If a customer asks for a figure you do not know, look it up or ask. Never invent one.
